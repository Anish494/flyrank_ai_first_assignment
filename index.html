Content Archetypes: A Behavioral Clustering Approach to Search Content Triage

Grouping 28,311 pages into five behavioral archetypes to help a content team decide what to review first — built and validated on FlyRank's anonymized search performance data.

By Anish Machine Learning Track — FlyRank AI Internship 2026

Abstract

Five-sentence summary

This project asks whether FlyRank's search content inventory naturally separates into a small number of behavioral archetypes, rather than requiring one hand-written scoring rule for every page. Using five observable signals — impressions, average position, click-through rate, word count, and content age — I fit a K-Means model (K=5, chosen by silhouette score) on 28,311 pages from the starter content-refresh dataset. The resulting archetypes range from a small, high-traffic "Champions" group (1.3% of pages) to a large "Underperforming Long-Form" group (41.4%), and comparing them against a simple declining-page baseline rule showed the baseline under-flags exactly the weakest-performing archetype. A grouped-by-client validation check confirmed the clustering is broadly stable across unseen clients, though measurably less stable than an earlier random-split check suggested. The result is a ranked, reason-coded content action playbook intended for human-reviewed triage, not automated decision-making.

1 — Introduction

The problem: one rule can't describe every page

A content team with hundreds of thousands of pages and limited review capacity needs a way to decide what to look at first. The natural first instinct is a hand-written rule — for example, "flag any page that is declining and still gets meaningful traffic." That works, but it treats every flagged page identically, even though a page that's declining because it's stuck on page 5 of search results needs a very different response than a page that's declining because a related page cannibalized its traffic.

This project asks a narrower, more tractable question: rather than trying to predict which pages will decline (a future outcome, and a much harder labeling problem), can we describe the current behavioral shape of the content inventory well enough to route different kinds of pages to different actions? That reframes the task from prediction to description — from classification to clustering.

Decision this supports: a content strategist deciding where to spend their next hour of review time, choosing among protect, promote, expand, rewrite, or prune actions across a large page inventory.

Cost of a wrong recommendation: if a page is routed to the wrong action — say, a page recommended for pruning that actually carries real traffic from an unusual angle — the cost is a wasted or counterproductive editorial decision, not a catastrophic one. This is why every recommendation in this project is framed as decision-support for a human reviewer, never as an automatic action (see Section 6).

3 — Methodology

Framing, features, and how K was chosen

Task type: clustering, not classification. There is no historical "correct archetype" label to predict — the archetypes themselves are the thing being discovered. The "target" is the cluster assignment the algorithm produces, which only becomes meaningful once each cluster's contents are inspected and named.

Features (five, each independently justified as knowable at decision time): impressions (90-day observed traffic), average position (observed ranking), click-through rate (observed engagement), word count (static content property), and content age (computed from creation date). A pairwise correlation check confirmed all five carry largely independent information (correlations between -0.12 and 0.16) — none is a redundant restatement of another.

Choosing K

K was chosen by comparing silhouette score and inertia (elbow) across K = 2 through 8. Both signals pointed to the same answer.

Silhouette score by K. K=5 is a clear local peak (0.384), and inertia's rate of improvement visibly slows after this point.

Baseline

Before modeling, a simple hand-written rule was built as the thing any clustering approach needs to justify itself against: flag a page when it is both currently declining and still earning meaningful traffic (impressions_90d ≥ 500). Two candidate signals were checked before finalizing this rule — content staleness came back OPPOSITE of the expected direction (non-stale pages declined more often than stale ones in this sample), so the rule deliberately avoids leaning on staleness at all.

Validation design

Clustering has no train/test accuracy in the classification sense, so stability was checked instead: the same model was re-fit on two independently sampled halves of the data, first with a plain random row split, then with a grouped-by-client split (no client's pages allowed to appear in both halves). Results are reported honestly in Section 4.

Leakage audit performed: every feature was checked against a standard leakage checklist (calculated only from pre-decision information; not a rebuilt product flag; not redundant with another feature). The one place leakage was found was methodological, not feature-based — see the validation comparison below.

Where the baseline rule missed something

Applying the baseline rule (declining + ≥500 impressions) across an earlier clustering pass showed the flag rate varied enormously by archetype — from 0% to 41.1%. Most notably, the archetype with the objectively worst position and CTR had the lowest real flag rate (16.0%), likely because the baseline's impression floor filters out low-traffic pages before the decline check ever runs. In other words: the baseline may be systematically under-reviewing exactly the pages that need the most attention, simply because they are also the lowest-volume ones. Clustering surfaced this gap directly; the single binary baseline rule could not have revealed it on its own.

What this means: the random split made the clustering look almost perfectly stable — a gap of just 0.005. Once genuinely unseen clients were forced into separate halves, the real gap was roughly 7× larger. The clustering is still meaningfully stable (both grouped scores sit well above zero, close to the full-data silhouette of 0.384), but the random-split check overstated how clean that stability was. This mirrors a finding from the paper this project was benchmarked against (see Section 5) — random splits across a multi-client dataset can look better than they truly are.

5 — Limitations & Honest Framing

What this project can and cannot say

This project can say: that this content inventory, on this snapshot, separates into five behaviorally distinct, measurably stable archetypes based on observable signals — and that a simple baseline rule treats those archetypes very unevenly. These are observed, directional, decision-support findings.

This project cannot say: that any recommended action will cause a page's performance to improve; that cluster membership predicts future outcomes; that these archetypes are Google-ranking-algorithm categories; or that this clustering is stable across time (only cross-client stability was tested).

  • Single snapshot, no seasonality test. Archetype membership for a given page is not guaranteed to hold in a different month.
  • Missing word-count data. Rows with missing word counts were treated as zero during clustering, which may have nudged a small number of pages toward the wrong archetype.
  • Within-archetype outliers matter. A handful of "Buried & Weak" pages carry 30,000–59,000+ impressions despite deep positions — far above that archetype's average. Archetype membership alone understates their priority; a within-archetype ranking by traffic is needed on top of it.
  • No causal validation. This is an observational, descriptive analysis. Testing whether a recommended edit actually improves outcomes would require a controlled before/after experiment, outside this project's scope.

Benchmarked against FlyRank's own March 2026 portfolio research paper (341,701 content pieces, 57 brands), this project's approach mirrors that paper's own explicit caution around its exploratory K-Means appendix: both works treat cluster archetypes as descriptive lenses, not fixed operational personas, and both flag that validation design (grouped vs. random splits) materially changes how confident a result should be treated.

Human review is required before any action

For every recommended action, a reviewer should confirm the archetype label actually matches the page's real content and business context before acting.

What should never be automated

  1. Never auto-delete or auto-unpublish "prune" pages — some carry real value (backlinks, legal content) or, as shown in Section 5, unusually high traffic despite a weak position.
  2. Never auto-rewrite content using AI without human review of the output.
  3. Never treat "protect" as a reason to stop monitoring — champions can decline too.
  4. Never merge pages automatically based on archetype similarity alone — behavioral similarity is not topical similarity.
  5. Never use this ranking as a performance evaluation of the person who created the page.

Monitoring & retrain triggers

This playbook should be re-run when: any archetype's portfolio share shifts by more than ~10 percentage points; the rate of data-quality issues (impossible CTR, missing word count) rises above what was seen in this run; at least every 90 days regardless, since this is a single-snapshot analysis; or if a future grouped-split stability check shows the client-generalization gap widening beyond what was found here.
